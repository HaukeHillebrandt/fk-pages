Spoilers for Lacanian Psychoanalysis

On the tarmac at Heathrow the planeful of people waited silently to be taken into the air. The air hostess stood in the aisle and mimed with her props as the recording played. We were strapped into our seats, a field of strangers, in a silence like the silence of a congregation while the liturgy is read. She showed us the life jacket with its little pipe, the emergency exits, the oxygen mask dangling from a length of clear tubing. She led us through the possibility of death and disaster, as the priest leads the congregation through the details of purgatory and hell; and no one jumped up to escape while there was still time. Instead we listened or half-listened, thinking about other things, as though some special hardness had been bestowed on us by this coupling of formality with doom. When the recorded voice came to the part about the oxygen masks, the hush remained unbroken: no one protested, or spoke up to disagree with this commandment that one should take care of others only after taking care of oneself. Yet I wasn’t sure it was altogether true.

On one side of me sat a swarthy boy with lolling knees, whose fat thumbs sped around the screen of a gaming console. On the other was a small man in a pale linen suit, richly tanned, with a silver plume of hair. Outside, the turgid summer afternoon lay stalled over the runway; little airport vehicles raced unconstrained across the flat distances, skating and turning and circling like toys, and further away still was the silver thread of the motorway that ran and glinted like a brook bounded by the monotonous fields. The plane began to move, trundling forward so that the vista appeared to unfreeze into motion, flowing past the windows first slowly and then faster, until there was the feeling of effortful, half-hesitant lifting as it detached itself from the earth. There was a moment in which it seemed impossible that this could happen. But then it did.

I met this patient, Mr. F., on this airplane, where he was seated next to me. Or rather across from me, for I happened to fly business due to the generosity of some pathetic billionaire, who clearly wants to fuck me (as all men do), and flew me in to talk about psychodynamic therapy, all the while Mr. F. was merely upgraded from cattle class through serendipitous circumstance.

He let it slip that he was en route to a ‘writing retreat’ in northern California. ‘I hope you got an Electric Heated Jacket with USB Charging Heated Vest, Washable Warm Heat Jacket with 3 Optional Temperature and some HotHands Toe Warmers and are not on GLP-1s,’ he said. ‘SF will be very cold.’ I said I was afraid that was not the case, and he raised his eyebrows, which were starting to grey and grew unexpectedly wildly from his forehead, as if the hairs had left the distribution altogether: outliers in the deep sense, a representation of the errors in the genetic code that had been copied one too many times. It was this eccentricity that had made me answer him. The unexpected sometimes looks like a prompting of fate.

He evidently suffered from a recurring herpes in his lips, which, he explained, broke out whenever he met a woman who disagreed with him on any topic, a symptom his physician had attributed to stress, though he himself preferred to think of it as a reaction to jetlag.

He had asked me the reason for my visit to San Francisco. I said I was going there for work.

He immediately launched into his life story, giving me an OUTLINE of his whole sappy little life story. Having grown up in a small village in northern Germany (clearly with a chip on his shoulder about this, but also clearly trying to signal his street cred, but also clearly sheltered), he went on to embark on a mission of pseudointellectual academic credentialing. ‘You know, I actually used to live in the US. I taught a course at a college in Boston,’ he said rather proudly. ‘Well not in Boston, but nearby. No, not Tufts.’

I should not have revealed that I am a Lacanian psychoanalyst, for it encouraged him to sign up as a patient with me and come to my office every week and drone on and on, talking my ear off. He almost immediately got into the habit of monologuing—as all male patients do. They’re all narcissists who are in love with their pathetic little man words. Men. Ugh. The whole earth is sick of your search for knowledge. (Men, can’t live with them, can’t live without them aminotrite ladies?).

In one session, I prompted him to talk about his dating history (big mistake). He said that he had always been a ladies’ man and didn’t have to brag, and had kissed women from all the important continents including Oceania. Kissed et cetera, he said, and moved his hand as though waving a car through a checkpoint.

Of the dozens of women he had kissed, he had been in love with only a few. But love was no longer his word for it. He called it limerence, a term he had come across some years ago and preferred now, since it named a state rather than a relation and could therefore be undergone alone. It arrived every three to four years. He had only lately noticed this pattern, and it had disturbed him to find that something he experienced with such explosiveness had such a regularity. Indeed, he frequently complained about gastrointestinal disturbances, the symptoms of which were entirely orthogonal to the regularity of his limerence pattern. “Explosive diarrhea in the street, anal retentive in the sheets,” I wrote on my notepad.

The question he had come with was whether he ought to date without the feeling. Settle. ‘No more Feeld. Get back on the horse, get back on Hinge, find a wife’. He had tried it. There had been women who were good on paper – ‘and I mean that as a compliment,’ he said, turning his watch around on his wrist – but looking back, those relationships seemed to him finished before they had started, and finished by him, by his conviction that he ought to be more in love than he was. He would lie beside a perfectly nice person, he said, and audit himself. Twice it had been different. Twice there had been little feeling at the outset and he had come to appreciate the woman slowly, he said, the way you come to like a town you were posted to rather than one you chose. In both cases the women ended it, a fact that he was clearly still bitter about.

In the courtyard someone was dragging bins across the pavement (this was before my office moved to Fleet Street), and he waited for the noise to stop.

The women he fell for were another matter, and here his manner became more exact. It was never wholly unrequited limerence from afar: he wanted that understood. Something always happened. They slept together, and not just once, so that it could not be put down to accident. ‘You’re not talking to someone who woke up a loser,’ he said. But, alas, he could not hold them. Had they been simply slightly out of his league? He found this consoling, because it let him account for the pain in economic terms. There had been a real chance, he said, a small one; I wasn’t deluded; what hurt was having come so close to a return that was never very likely. He was silent for a moment. Then again, he said, it’s possible the odds were the attraction.

I said that when my daughter was small she had been devoted to a machine on the pier, a glass box in which a metal claw descended on a heap of soft toys. The claw would close on one, lift it, carry it most of the way to the chute and then let go. The same toys were sold in the shop next door for less than she spent, and she had never once asked for one.

He turned the watch back round. ‘The two who weren’t like that, though,’ he said. ‘The ones from the shop. They left as well.’

I said that perhaps they had seen him looking at the machine.

And that neuroscientists have long understood that there’s a certain je ne sais quoi associated with variable reward learning. Noticing the puzzled look on his face, I half-sang a snatch of Katy Perry:

You're hot, then you're cold

You're yes, then you're no

You're in, then you're out

You're up, then you're down (Down)

Someone call the doctor

Got a case of a love bipolar

Stuck on a roller coaster

Can't get off this ride

When I suggested that his grief over his most recent girlfriend ending things closely resembled the fury of an infant whose rattle has been placed on a high shelf, he stood up, straightened his cuffs with great dignity, and announced that he did not believe in Lacanian psychoanalysis—a conviction he had somehow neglected to mention while paying 80 quid per session over the two years, most of which I had ended early.

He, fucking finally, ended it after two years, after I pulled pretty much every Lacanian trick, I’m serious this guy was so persistent, he came back week after week, asking for more humiliation.
